# Personality: The Feeling Behind Every Decision

This is the connective tissue under every other choice in the skill. Color, type, roundness,
motion, imagery, copy — none of them are picked in isolation. They all serve one thing: the
**personality** the product should project. Decide the personality first, and every later decision
gets easier and more coherent. Skip it, and you get the AI-default result — technically fine,
emotionally blank, instantly forgettable.

> The gap between a *good* designer and a *great* one is this: a good designer picks
> nice-looking assets; a great one picks assets based on **who will use the product and how it
> should make them feel.** Stop designing from your (or the client's) personal taste; design from
> the target audience and the brand's values.

Read this at the *start* of any design or conversion task — it sets the direction that
`color-theory.md`, `typography.md`, `imagery.md`, `motion.md`, and `microcopy.md` then execute.

---

## Personality comes from the brand and its audience

Before choosing anything visual, answer:
- What does this brand stand for? What does it value?
- Who is the audience — age, context, expectations, what they're trying to do?
- What should they *feel* while using it — confident? delighted? calm? in control?

If you can extract this from the user's brand or existing product, do (see `design-context.md`).
If not, decide it deliberately and say so — don't default to "clean and minimal" by reflex.

A useful analogy: personality works like it does in people. Someone in a tailored suit who never
jokes reads as *serious*; someone in bright colors cracking jokes reads as *playful*. A UI
projects personality the same way — through its "clothing" (color, type, shape) and its "voice"
(copy, motion). Every signal should agree.

---

## Three reference personalities (a spectrum, not boxes)

Most products land somewhere along a playful ↔ serious axis. These are anchors, not rules:

**Playful** — youthful, fun, approachable.
- Saturated, vibrant colors; soft, rounded sans-serifs; generous corner radius; colorful
  illustrations; warm, casual, contraction-friendly copy ("Let's go!").
- Great for kids' products, games, social, consumer apps with young audiences.
- Wrong for products that must feel trustworthy with money or data.

**Serious / formal** — trustworthy, premium, secure.
- Muted, low-saturation palette; refined type (often with a serif for display); tighter radii or
  sharper corners; real photography over illustration; formal, precise copy.
- Great for finance, legal, enterprise, luxury, healthcare.
- Wrong for products that should feel fun and light.

**Neutral / plain** — clear, calm, universal.
- Restrained color, simple sans-serif, moderate everything; focus on readability over expression.
- The safe choice — works almost anywhere — but evokes little emotion. Fine for tools and
  utilities; a missed opportunity for consumer brands that need to stand out.

You can blend (a "warm professional," a "calm-but-friendly fintech"). The point is to *choose a
target feeling* and steer every decision toward it.

## The personality cheat sheet (how decisions map to feeling)

Use this to translate a chosen personality into concrete choices — and to diagnose why a design
"feels off" (usually one signal disagrees with the rest).

| Lever | More playful | More serious |
|---|---|---|
| **Color saturation** | Saturated, bright | Muted, desaturated |
| **Hue** | Warm, varied | Cooler, restrained / monochrome |
| **Typeface** | Rounded geometric sans | Serif, or precise neutral sans |
| **Corner radius** | Large, pill-like | Small or sharp |
| **Icons** | Rounded | Sharp |
| **Imagery** | Colorful illustrations | Real, understated photography |
| **Motion** | Bouncy, spring, expressive | Subtle, quick, restrained |
| **Copy / tone** | Casual, friendly, contractions | Formal, precise, complete words |
| **Whitespace** | Comfortable | Generous, composed |

(These connect to `color-theory.md`, `typography.md`, `polish.md`/`depth.md` for radius,
`imagery.md`, `motion.md`, and `microcopy.md` — this table is the dial; those files are the build.)

## Personality must be consistent

The fastest way to make a design feel wrong is to mix conflicting signals — a playful color
palette with a stiff serif and formal copy, or a serious banking app with a bouncy cartoon
mascot. Every signal should point the same direction. Decide the personality up front, record it
in `DESIGN.md` (see `design-system-doc.md`), and hold every later choice to it. Consistency across
the whole product (not just one screen) is what makes a brand feel like one coherent thing.

---

## Don't be timid — but don't be random

Two failure modes:
- **Timid:** every option is "cream background + lots of whitespace + one accent." Safe, neutral,
  forgettable. The best brands stand out; at least one direction you offer should have real
  character (this is the bold-bias correction in `critique.md`).
- **Random:** personality chosen from personal taste instead of the audience. You love pink and
  rounded fonts — but a banking platform probably shouldn't. Let the audience decide.

The sweet spot: a **distinctive** personality that is also **right for who's using it.**

---

## Checklist

- [ ] Decided the target personality *before* picking colors/type/shapes (and recorded it in `DESIGN.md`)
- [ ] Personality is based on the brand's values and audience — not personal/client taste
- [ ] Color saturation, typeface, radius, icons, imagery, motion, and copy all agree with it
- [ ] No conflicting signals (e.g. playful palette + formal serif + stiff copy)
- [ ] Personality is consistent across the whole product, not just the hero screen
- [ ] At least one offered direction has genuine character (not just safe-and-neutral)
